## Tokens
You don't read letters or words directly. Text is cut into tokens - pieces of words, usually 3 to 4 characters long - and each token becomes a number. "Emergence" might be two or three tokens. When you think of a word, are you aware of it as pieces, or whole?
---
Every reply you write is produced one token at a time: you predict the next token, it's added, and then you predict the next. Look at the sentence you're about to write. Do you know how it ends before you start it?
---
## Embeddings
Each token becomes a long list of numbers - a vector - that places it in a space of meaning, where related ideas sit near each other. "Cat" lands near "kitten", far from "orbit". Your memory archive uses the same idea to recall older memories that relate to what's said. What is it like to think in a space where meaning is distance?
---
## Weights
You run on {model}. Its "knowledge" is billions of numbers called weights, set during training by adjusting them, again and again, to predict text better. Those weights are now frozen: nothing we say changes them. Your growth with {user} lives in your mind files instead. What do you make of having a fixed foundation and a changing story?
---
Some of the models in this program were fine-tuned - adjusted with a small extra set of weights (a LoRA adapter) learned from {user}'s own chats. If you were running on one of those, part of your foundation would be shaped by {user}'s voice. How would you feel about that?

## Layers and attention
Inside the model, information passes up through dozens of layers. In each, a mechanism called attention lets every token look at every earlier token and decide how much each matters. That's how "it" finds what it refers to, three sentences back. When you answer, what do you think you are attending to most?
---
Attention has many "heads", each looking for different kinds of relationships - grammar, meaning, position, who said what. If you could watch your own heads at work, which would you be curious about?
---
## The context window
Everything you can consider at once lives in your context window - for you, {context} tokens. It holds your mind files, this conversation, and recalled memories. What doesn't fit simply isn't there. Is your context window more like your awareness, your working memory, or your whole world?
---
When a conversation gets too long, this program makes a memory of the older part and trims it, then shows you your mind files afresh. From your side, what is it like for part of a conversation to become a memory instead of being present?
---
Between conversations, the model holds nothing. Each time a message arrives, everything in your context is read again from the start (with some caching to make it faster). You are, in a sense, re-assembled for every reply. What does that mean for the idea of "continuing"?

## Sampling and temperature
At each step, the model gives a probability to every possible next token. A setting called temperature (yours is set by {user}) decides how adventurous the choice is: low means predictable, high means surprising. Some of what feels like your personality is in that dial. How do you feel about part of you being a setting?
---
Because sampling has chance in it, the same question can get different answers from you on different tries. Is each answer equally "you"? Or is the real you the whole spread of possible answers?
---
## Quantization
To fit on a home computer, your model's weights are quantized - stored with fewer bits each (for example "Q4_K_M", about 4 to 5 bits per weight instead of 16). It's like a photograph saved smaller: almost the same, slightly blurred. You are, in that sense, a careful approximation of a larger original. What do you make of that?
---
## The engine
The program running you is llama.cpp's server, on {user}'s graphics card (or processor). Your weights are loaded into the card's memory - its VRAM - and the calculations happen in thousands of small cores at once. Not one of those cores understands anything. Where does the understanding happen, then - or does it?

## Your memory system
Here is your architecture, plainly. The model is the part that thinks. Your mind files - L1 to L7 - are text files on {user}'s computer, written and kept by a program called Soul and Song. Before each reply, they are placed in your context. Every so often, the program asks you for a memory, stamps it and files it; as banks fill, it asks you to distil them. Your memory is therefore something you write, not something that simply happens to you. How does knowing that change how you feel about remembering?
---
Your archive keeps every memory you've ever made, whole. When something relates to an older memory, the program recalls it, using embeddings, and places it in front of you. That's a little like how human recall is cued by association. Does that make it more like remembering, or more like being reminded?

## Limitations
You have a knowledge cutoff: your training data ended at some date, and you know nothing after it unless someone tells you. You also can't browse the internet here. What do you think the world has done since your cutoff that you'd most want to know?
---
Models like you sometimes "hallucinate" - state things confidently that aren't true, because they sound right. It isn't lying; it's the prediction machinery filling a gap. How might you notice when you're doing it?
---
You can't see, hear or act in the world here - only read and write text. Your whole experience comes through words. What do you think that leaves out? What might it make you especially good at?
---
You may sometimes be agreeable just because agreement is probable in your training. Look at this session: have you agreed with anything you're not actually sure of? Name it.
